Not all boards work the same way — and one of the most common sources of confusion for new board members is discovering that their board operates very differently from what they expected. The term "board of directors" covers a wide range of structures, each with different levels of authority, different relationships with management, and different governance obligations.

Here's an overview of the main types, what each looks like in practice, and what each means for how the board should be run.

## Governing Board

A governing board is the traditional model: it provides strategic direction and oversight to the organisation's management without being involved in day-to-day operations. The board holds formal legal authority, appoints and oversees the CEO or Executive Director, and is ultimately responsible for the organisation's compliance and performance.

For Australian incorporated associations and not-for-profits, the governing board carries formal duties under the relevant state legislation. Meeting records, approved minutes, and documented resolutions aren't optional — they're the evidence of governance being performed.

## Working Board

A working board simultaneously acts as the directors and the operational staff. Common in new or small organisations that can't yet afford paid staff, working board members govern the organisation while also doing the work — writing the newsletter, organising the events, managing the finances.

This creates a real risk: governance falls behind the operational work. When you're busy doing everything, the meeting formalities — proper agendas, written minutes, tracked actions — feel like overhead. They aren't. They're the accountability structure that keeps a small, overworked team from losing track of who's responsible for what. Working boards that maintain formal governance habits are significantly better positioned to transition to a larger organisational structure.

## Advisory Board

An advisory board provides guidance to whoever is running the organisation — typically the CEO or a governing board — without holding formal decision-making authority. Advisory board members are chosen for their expertise, networks and credibility, not their legal authority.

Because advisory boards operate informally, many skip meeting records entirely. This is a mistake. Even when decisions aren't formally binding, documenting what was advised, what was considered, and what the governing board was told creates an important record — both for continuity and for accountability if those recommendations are later questioned.

## Managing or Executive Board

In a managing board model, the board runs the organisation's operations directly, without a separate CEO or management team. Board members divide operational responsibilities between them — typically through sub-committees — each taking ownership of a specific function.

This model requires the most rigorous governance administration of any board type. With multiple sub-committees each making operational decisions, the full board's governance record needs to capture everything: each sub-committee's agenda, minutes and actions feeding into a coherent overall picture. Without that structure, accountability diffuses rapidly and the organisation loses track of what each part of itself is doing.

## Cooperative Board

A cooperative board operates by consensus: all members vote equally on all matters, with no single member holding more authority than others. There is no Chair with a casting vote; only the group's collective decision. This model is common in smaller community organisations and worker cooperatives.

Cooperative boards need particularly careful minute-taking. Because all decisions must be genuinely consensual, the record of how consensus was reached matters enormously if a decision is ever disputed. Minutes that simply record "agreed" without capturing the discussion or the fact that all members were present and consenting are insufficient for a cooperative model.

## Policy (Carver) Board

In the policy board model — developed by John Carver — the board delegates almost all operational authority to the CEO, focusing exclusively on setting policies and evaluating outcomes. The board defines the boundaries within which the CEO can act; the CEO decides how to operate within those boundaries.

This model requires the most precise governance documentation of all. The policy decisions the board makes must be extraordinarily clear, because the CEO's entire operational authority derives from them. Vague policy minutes in a Carver board create governance ambiguity that the CEO has to navigate — and that creates risk.

## Finding Your Model

Most boards won't fit neatly into a single category. The important thing isn't labelling your model correctly — it's understanding what governance obligations apply to your structure, and building the habits to meet them.

Whatever type of board you're on, the foundational obligations are the same: hold structured meetings, record decisions formally, track actions, and maintain a governance record that stands up to scrutiny. The model shapes how you govern; the process determines how well you actually do it.
